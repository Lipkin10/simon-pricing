## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. Price is the strongest profit driver (ch05)
Profit = Price × Volume − Cost. In Simon's industrial base case (10 % return on sales), improving each driver by 5 % in isolation gives: **price +50 % profit, variable cost +30 %, volume +20 %, fixed cost +15 %**. Managers spend their time in the inverse order. With volume constant, **Δprofit % ≈ Δprice % ÷ net margin %**: a 2 % increase lifts profit ~41 % at Walmart-like margins. Price is also the only instrument with zero upfront cost and near-instant effect — and the one competitors neutralize fastest.

### 2. Every price cut has a break-even volume — compute it first (ch05, ch09)
Required volume gain = cut % ÷ (contribution margin % − cut %). A 20 % cut on a 40 % contribution margin needs **+100 % volume**; managers' spontaneous guess ("+20 %") loses money. Typical price elasticities are **1.3–3 (median ≈ 2)**, so any break-even that needs more than that is a fantasy. At a 6 % net margin, a 3 % discount means doing twice the work for the same profit.

### 3. Cut volume, not price (ch09)
Same 5 % shock: price −5 % → profit −50 %; volume −5 % → profit −20 %, because lost units also save variable cost. In a crisis attack capacity first (shifts, lines, unmade product), and give **goods instead of price** when a concession is unavoidable — the customer values the gift at retail, you pay variable cost, and it withdraws itself.

### 4. Price = value; cost-plus and competitor-following are shortcuts (ch02, ch06)
Willingness to pay tracks *perceived* value. Three management tasks: **create, communicate, retain value**. Cost-plus ignores what the customer would pay; pegging to a competitor outsources your pricing to a rival with a different cost structure. In B2B, quantify second-order outcomes in money over the asset's life (GE: 15-year energy savings) and bring that number to the table.

### 5. Share value fifty-fifty (ch06)
With linear demand, **optimal price = ½ (maximum price + variable unit cost)**. Pass on **half** of any cost, tax, currency or value change; a 20 % value advantage earns a **10 %** premium. Being within a few percent of the optimum costs little (±$5 on $105 → −4.8 %); far off costs a lot (±$15 → −42.9 %). Never set a price without a numeric demand curve from **at least two methods** (expert judgment + conjoint preferred; historical data is usually useless).

### 6. One price captures ~half the profit triangle (ch07)
A uniform price leaves roughly 50 % of attainable profit with buyers who would pay more and buyers priced out above variable cost. Differentiate by person, quantity (nonlinear: first unit dearest), bundle, time, place — **only with a fence**. Without one, a second price can land *below* the one-price result (power tool: −43 %). Each added tier gains less (+33 % → +12.5 %); stop when information and control cost exceed the gain. As seller quote **incremental** volume discounts, never full-volume.

### 7. Structure beats level: change the metric (ch08)
Innovation comes from what you charge for, when, and who sets it: pay-per-use (Michelin per mile turns +25 % performance into +25 % revenue automatically), new price metrics (per m² of finished wall instead of per ton), two-part tariffs (BahnCard: 50 % perceived, <30 % real discount), freemium (upside is in the free/paid gap, not the price; elasticity ≈ 1), intelligent surcharges on low-attention elements. Flat rates only where consumption is naturally capped. Never attach a fee to something customers feel is already paid for without research.

### 8. Psychology: anchors, the middle, three options (ch03)
Buyers without a reference adopt any anchor put in front of them (experts moved 32 % by a stray number). Most pick the **middle** of a range — widen the top to move the middle up. Inserting a **third option** shifts share to higher tiers with no price change (telecom ARPU +23 %, bank +28 %). Price signals quality when quality is hard to judge; a "too low" price can block sales (fix with a richer package, not a cut). Treat behavioral effects as exceptions (~5 % of cases) to test, not laws.

### 9. Position is DNA, not a dial (ch04)
Low, ultra-low, premium and luxury each have explicit success-factor lists; one missing structural factor disqualifies the position. No known case of a mid/high-price firm becoming a successful low-price firm. Answer low-price attackers with a ~20 % cheaper second brand (LEA), not a lead-brand cut. "Better before cheaper" is the rule of the 0.7 % of firms with lasting top-decile returns. Premium needs superior objective value plus heavy value communication and rare promotions.

### 10. Discipline: floors, incentives, the CEO's calendar (ch05, ch08, ch10)
Any approval threshold becomes the number deals settle at (20 % floor → 20.1 % deals). Pay salespeople on realized price or discount level (commission rises as discount falls; show it live) — a $14 B firm cut average discount 16 % → 14 % in three months with no volume loss. Pay executives on shareholder value, never on price. Keep the CEO out of routine negotiations. Track relative prices at every aggregation level. Sustained +2 % price ≈ +26.7 % market cap at PE ~18.

### 11. Avoid the catastrophic mistake before seeking the optimal strategy (ch10)
Bad price moves hit the share price at once; good ones pay slowly. Never: cut from strength against no-names (Marlboro Friday −26 %), build identity on a discount slogan (Praktiker → bankruptcy), raise 60 % justified by your own costs (Netflix −75 %), swap the price model untested (J.C. Penney −65 %), or break an oligopoly's price peace for volume (potash −24 % in two days).

### 12. Promotions borrow from the future (ch02, ch05)
After any volume spike ask where the buyers came from. A mirror-image dip in the following months (GM 2005: +41 % June, −24 % September, $10.5 B loss) means demand was pulled forward, not created. When promotions exceed ~half of category sales, you are discounting what would have sold anyway.

## Audit Protocol — applying Simon to a pricing artifact

When asked to audit a rate card, discount policy, proposal or pricing spreadsheet, check in this order and report each as claim → evidence in the artifact → Simon's rule → consequence in the artifact's own numbers:

1. **Method**: is price derived from cost (cost-plus), from competitors, or from customer value / willingness to pay? Which inputs exist for value? (F4, F5)
2. **Leverage**: run the 2 %/5 % test on the artifact's own margin: Δprofit ≈ Δprice ÷ margin. (F1)
3. **Discounts**: list every discount, its trigger and its stacking rule; compute break-even volume for each and for the stack; compare with elasticity 1.3–3; check whether any floor makes the discount grid self-contradictory. (F2, F10)
4. **Floors and thresholds**: what number will deals converge on? (F10)
5. **Concessions in goods vs price**: are payment-term and closing discounts given as price when they could be given as scope, credit or time? (F3)
6. **Structure**: single metric or differentiated? Is there a fence? Is there a third option / anchor? Does the metric capture the value delivered (time vs outcome)? (F6, F7, F8)
7. **Positioning**: does the price signal the position claimed (premium below benchmark = mixed signal)? (F8, F9)
8. **Incentives**: who is paid on what? (F10)
9. **Catastrophe scan**: any move on the ch10 list? (F11)

For a specific market, combine with measured elasticity and the artifact's own numbers. The book states no Amoroso-Robinson formula; the midpoint rule is the one Simon commits to.
