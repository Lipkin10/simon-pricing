# Chapter 4: Price Positioning: High or Low

## Core Idea
Price position (low, ultra-low, premium, luxury) is a founding-level strategic choice that locks in business model, cost culture, segments and channels; every position can win or fail, and switching later almost never works.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Success Factors for a Low-Price Strategy** (11 factors), **Ultra-low Price Strategy** (6), **Premium Price Strategy** (7), **Luxury Goods Price Strategies** (8): see checklists below.
  - When to use: before committing a new company, brand or second brand to a position; as an audit of whether an existing position is coherent.
  - How: score the candidate position against every factor; one missing structural factor (e.g. no day-one cost culture for low price) disqualifies the position.
- **Less Expensive Alternative (LEA)**: a stripped, second-branded product placed ~20% below the lead brand to answer low-price imitators without cutting the main brand.
  - When to use: imitators attack from below and a price cut on the lead brand would destroy margin across the portfolio.
  - How: second brand; minimal service, no customization, bulk-only delivery, longer lead times (7–20 days); price gap ~20%; measure complementarity vs. cannibalization.
- **"Better before cheaper" / "revenue before cost"** (Raynor & Ahmed): the two rules shared by the 0.7% of firms that stayed top-decile ROA every listed year.
  - When to use: when choosing between differentiation and cost leadership as the profit engine.
  - How: default to differentiation plus gross-margin advantage; treat cost leadership as an exception that requires being one of the 1–2 largest players.
- **Ex-ante price-and-volume setting for limited editions**: set the unit cap and the price together, before launch, and never breach the cap.

## Key Concepts
- **Price positioning**: the deliberate choice of where a firm's prices sit relative to the market, made once and rarely reversible.
- **Low price–high profit club**: the short list of firms (Aldi, IKEA, H&M/Zara, Ryanair, Dell) whose extreme efficiency turns low prices into above-sector margins.
- **Everyday low price vs. hi-lo**: constant low prices vs. frequent temporary promotions; winners never mix the two.
- **Ultra-low price segment**: emerging-market price points 50–70% below developed-market low prices, serving the 86% of humanity with family income under $10,000.
- **Reverse innovation**: developing simplified products in emerging markets and later selling them in rich markets.
- **Value to customer**: perceived value or utility; the only justification for a premium, and the only thing that counts once perceived.
- **Prestige / snob / Veblen effects**: luxury demand drivers where a higher price can raise, not lower, volume.
- **Massification**: degradation of an exclusive brand into mass status through volume growth (Lacoste, Opel after the Kadett).
- **Halo effect**: value a flagship luxury product radiates onto the parent brand (Bugatti Veyron for VW).
- **Gray market**: original goods resold at steep discounts through uncontrolled channels; toxic to luxury residual value.
- **Hybrid consumer**: buyer who discounts on groceries to afford a three-star dinner; segments are not neat.

## Mental Models
- Think of price position as DNA, not a dial: it is set at founding and expressed in culture; a mid-price firm cannot become Aldi.
- Use "acceptable, not minimal" as the value floor for low and ultra-low positions: cut everything the customer does not require, stop exactly where they would refuse or switch.
- Think of premium as a price-value ratio contest and luxury as a prestige contest: premium wins on objective value at a fair gap; luxury wins on scarcity, image and willingness to pay, with cost control almost irrelevant.
- Use a new price metric (per hour of light, per kilometer) when customers will not do the total-cost-of-ownership math themselves.

## Anti-patterns
- **Switching to low price from a mid/high position**: the author knows no successful case; cost culture cannot be retrofitted.
- **Meeting imitators by cutting the lead brand's price**: destroys portfolio margin; use an LEA instead.
- **Defending premium by ignoring the low end**: when premium is 4% of units (Chinese injection molding), competitors with acceptable quality attack from below; extend range downward instead.
- **Developing ultra-low products in a high-wage country**: an illusion; whole value chain (R&D, procurement, manufacturing) must sit in the emerging market.
- **Pricing life-cycle savings into an up-front premium** (energy-saving bulbs at $20, electric scooters): customers judge at face value, cheap imports anchor low, low-interest categories reject premiums.
- **Growing a hi-lo promotion habit in premium/luxury**: too frequent or too steep and the position collapses; luxury must never discount even in crisis.
- **Chasing volume in luxury**: Maybach failed with unlimited supply at $650k; Bugatti sold out 300 units at $1.7M by capping first.
- **Launching a service-intensive luxury product**: worldwide white-glove service for a limited-run car costs more than any firm will bear.
- **Owning all luxury retail without noticing fixed-cost drift**: dealer commissions (variable) become rent and staff (fixed), raising break-even against a strategy built on low volume.
- **Underpricing a limited edition**: Swiss watch capped at 1,000 units, priced $32,000, drew 3,500 orders; $40,000 would have added $8M profit.

## Success-Factor Checklists
**Low price**
- Low-price, high-volume from day one, usually via a new business model
- Extreme cost and process efficiency
- Adequate and consistent quality
- No-frills focus on core products
- Growth and scale exploited fully
- Tough (fair) procurement
- Little or no debt; self-financing and supplier credit
- Own brands, control of the value chain
- Advertising almost only about price
- Everyday low price, never hi-lo
- Accept that the market holds only one or two of you

**Ultra-low price**
- Simple yet robust: bare essentials, never dysfunctional
- Develop in the emerging market
- Lowest-cost production locked in by design and location
- New, cheap marketing and sales channels
- Easy to use, easy to fix
- Consistent quality

**Premium**
- Superior objective value
- Price-value relationship as the competitive advantage
- Innovation, breakthrough or continuous ("forever better")
- Consistent high quality in product and service
- Strong brand turning temporary tech lead into lasting image
- Heavy investment in communicating value
- Rare, restrained promotions

**Luxury**
- Highest performance on every dimension: materials, quality, service, communication, distribution
- Prestige built in and conveyed
- Price used as prestige signal and quality indicator
- Strict volume and market-share caps, honored limited editions
- Zero discounts or special offers
- Top talent end to end, down to sales staff appearance
- Control of the value chain incl. distribution, watching fixed-cost creep
- Willingness to pay drives price; variable cost is secondary

## Worked Example
Honda held 90% of Vietnam's motorbike market with the Dream at ~$2,100. Chinese entrants priced at $550–700 took the market: Honda's volume fell from ~1M to 170,000 while the Chinese sold over 1M a year. A price cut to $1,300 stopped the bleed but was unsustainable and still double the Chinese price. Honda then built a new bike, the Wave, from cost-reduced local parts and global purchasing, with acceptable quality and minimal manufacturing cost, launched at $732 (65% below the old Dream). Honda regained the market and most Chinese rivals exited. Lesson: a premium player can win the ultra-low segment, but only with a redesigned product, local value chain and radical simplification, not by discounting the existing product.

## Key Takeaways
1. Pick the position at founding; treat any later switch, especially toward low price, as near-impossible.
2. Low price pays only with a clear, large, sustainable cost advantage and a frugal culture; expect 1–2 winners per market.
3. Answer low-price attackers with a ~20% cheaper second brand (LEA) or a down-market redesign, never a lead-brand cut.
4. Cover the segment sizes: if premium is 4% of units, extend downward with local R&D and production or be attacked from below.
5. Premium requires superior objective value plus heavy communication; test the value (Cayman) rather than following industry ratios.
6. Guarantees priced on true reliability (Enercon 97% vs actual 99%) cost nothing and cut buyer resistance.
7. In luxury, fix volume and price together up front, never discount, protect residual value and watch fixed costs from owned distribution.
