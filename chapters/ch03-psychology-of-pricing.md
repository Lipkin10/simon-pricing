# Chapter 3: The Strange Psychology of Pricing

## Core Idea
Buyers are neither the rational utility-maximizers of classical economics nor as irrational as behavioral economists claim: price carries prestige, quality and pain signals, and the *structure* of an offer (anchors, number of options, payment metric) moves revenue as much as the price level itself. Use these effects, but treat them as exceptions to test, not laws to assume.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Prestige (Veblen/"snob") effect**: within a range, the demand curve slopes upward — a higher price raises unit sales because price itself signals status.
  - When to use: premium/luxury goods, strong brands, where price is visible and nonnegotiable.
  - How: (1) find out whether an upward-sloping stretch exists; (2) never price inside it — the optimum sits above it, where the curve turns downward again; (3) if unsure, raise price stepwise and observe; (4) pair the increase with a design/packaging upgrade.
- **Price as an indicator of quality**: buyers read "higher price = higher quality" when they cannot judge quality directly.
  - When to use: new or rarely bought products, low absolute price, low price transparency, time pressure, fixed (non-bargained) prices.
  - How: diagnose whether a low price is *blocking* sales (quality doubt); if so, add features and re-bundle at a higher price into a conventional price-value frame rather than discount further.
- **Placebo effect of price**: a higher price changes not just perception but measured performance/experience.
- **Price anchor effect**: buyers with no reference point adopt whatever number is placed in front of them — consciously or not, professionals included.
  - How: put a high reference in the assortment or the conversation before the target price appears; the anchor need never sell.
- **Magic of the middle**: with no knowledge and no special requirement, buyers pick the middle of the price range.
  - How: widen the *top* of the range to pull the middle up; add a cheaper item to move a formerly cheapest item into the "middle". Avoid extreme anchors at both ends (top scares budget buyers, bottom triggers quality doubt).
- **Selling more by offering additional alternatives**: inserting a third option (decoy at the same price, or a mid-tier plan) shifts share to higher-priced options without any price change. Explanations: "magic of zero", anchoring, uncertainty about usage, quality expectation, risk avoidance, status.
- **Price thresholds and odd prices**: a threshold is a kink in the demand curve at round numbers ($1, $5, $10, $100).
  - How: if you decide to stay under a threshold, go as close as possible ($9.99, never $9.90/$9.95). If you cannot prove the threshold exists, test crossing it — reseller margins make the 99¢→$1.00 step disproportionately profitable.
- **Prospect theory** (Kahneman & Tversky): gains and losses have diminishing marginal utility, but a loss hurts more than an equal gain pleases; how the net result is *reached* matters, not just the net.
  - Derived effects: endowment effect, sunk-cost "earn it back" urge, cash-vs-card pain asymmetry, cash back, moon prices, price metric and installment design.
- **Mental accounting** (Thaler): spending is booked to separate mental accounts, each with its own price sensitivity and loss curve.
  - How: position an offer so it lands in the account where the buyer is least price-sensitive (Simon paid ~3× more for a car seat than an office chair without hesitation).
- **Neuro-pricing**: MRI measurement of price stimuli; price activates the pain center. Practical yield so far: only on price *display*.

## Key Concepts
- **Satisficing** (H. A. Simon): buyers settle for a good-enough outcome because information processing capacity is limited.
- **Price as a defused weapon**: where prestige/quality/placebo effects exist, cutting price fails and may lower share; unknown brands cannot buy their way in.
- **Cost-plus mentality of buyers**: consumers assume price tracks the seller's costs, so a low price implies cheap inputs.
- **Endowment effect**: owners ask far more for a good than non-owners will pay for the identical item.
- **Reminder effect of cash**: cash gives a visible running total of spend; card payments blur it.
- **Moon prices**: list prices nobody pays; they enable price differentiation and manufacture discount joy — until overuse destroys their credibility as anchors.
- **Price metric**: the unit in which the price is expressed (per month vs per year) — separate from how it is *collected*.
- **Payment depreciation**: the further a payment recedes, the weaker the urge to consume what was paid for.
- **Loss aversion**: the drive to avoid or postpone a loss (explains holding falling stocks too long).

## Mental Models
- Think of an anchor as a **product that earns without selling**: its job is to move willingness to pay, not to move units.
- Think of the assortment's price range as a **steering wheel**: you set where "the middle" is, and most uninformed buyers go there.
- Use **"price of the payment ≠ price of the metric"** when designing subscriptions: communicate in the smallest unit, collect in the fewest transactions — except where you need the customer to keep feeling the payment (fitness studios).
- Use **"pain in, joy out"** when structuring discounts: pay intangibly (card/transfer), refund tangibly (cash, stamps, negotiated rebate) — the same net price yields higher perceived utility.

## Anti-patterns
- **Discounting to enter a prestige market**: signals low quality; sales and share can fall (VW Phaeton, low-price cloud software).
- **Pricing on the upward slope**: leaving money on the table when the demand curve rises with price.
- **Reflexive 9-endings**: 43.5 % of FMCG prices end in 9 with no proof the threshold exists; the belief may be self-fulfilling marketing habit (Gabor & Granger 1964).
- **$9.90 / $9.95**: if you accept the threshold logic, these give away margin for nothing.
- **Shrinking the pack to dodge a threshold**: Skippy's indented jar and Häagen-Dazs's 14-oz "pint" drew public backlash.
- **Overused moon prices**: 82 %-off subscription offers stop functioning as anchors.
- **Generalizing lab findings**: most behavioral results come from laboratories with leading stimuli; annual-vs-monthly has no universal answer.
- **Extreme anchors**: an extremely high or low reference repels rather than steers.

## Numbers & Thresholds
- Chivas Regal: +20 % price, new label, identical whiskey → sales rose significantly.
- MediaShop cosmetic accessory: 29.90 € flopped; relaunched at 39.90 € (+33 %) → top seller, supply shortages.
- Electric razor: sharp price rise toward Braun → unit sales ×4.
- Cloud software: $19.90/seat/month vs competitor >$100 → large firms distrusted it; fix was a feature-rich higher-priced package.
- Placebo painkiller (vitamin C): high-price group 100 % reported relief, low-price group 50 %.
- Energy drink: $2.89 vs $0.89 → measurably better training and puzzle performance at the higher price.
- Used-car experts (n = 60): anchor $3,800 → estimate $3,563; anchor $2,800 → $2,520; gap $1,043 = 32 % of the $3,300 mean anchor.
- Sid & Harry suits: shouted $42, sold at $22. Schwarzenegger patio: argued $8,000, closed at $7,000.
- Padlock: range $4–$12 → bought $8; range $4–$16 → would have bought $10, +25 % revenue.
- Restaurant: entrées $10–$20, the $18 dish at 20 % share; add a $25 dish and the $18 share rises.
- Luggage: budget $200; show $900 anchor → buys $250–$300.
- Campbell's "Limit 12 per person" → 7 cans avg vs half that with "No limit".
- Bank (Test A → B): checking $1 / combo $2.50 → adding stand-alone card at $2.50 (2 % take) moved combo share 59 % → 81 %; revenue/customer $1.89 → $2.42 (+28 %); 1 M customers = $530 k/month, $6.36 M/year.
- Telecom: plans $25/$60 → 78 % cheap, ARPU $32.80; add $50 plan → 44 %/42 %/14 %, ARPU $40.50 (+23 %).
- Price endings: 43.5 % of 18,096 FMCG prices end in 9 (Kucher), none in 0; another study 25.9 %.
- Gas: $3.599 vs $3.60 on 20 gal = $71.98 vs $72.00 (2 ¢).
- Sparkling wine: only Mumm crossed 5 €; elasticity 3.64 vs much lower for rivals; ~50:50 split gives threshold elasticity ≈ 1.82.
- Women's clothing test: $34 / $39 / $44 → $39 sold most; $34 and $44 each 20 % lower.
- Resellers at 1 % return on sales: 99 ¢ → $1.00 doubles profit at constant volume; still positive with −10 % volume.
- Mug experiment: sellers asked $7.12, buyers offered $2.87 (mug worth ~$6).
- Lufthansa downgrade: refused 500 €, considered 1,500 € for 12 h; Boston $1,000 for 6.5 h = the same premium originally paid.
- Cash: 2/3 of purchases in cash in a 25,500-transaction study.
- Cash back: $30,000 car + $2,000 back beats $28,000 net.
- Green Stamps: 3 stamps/$ at 1 ¢ = 3 % discount; 150 stamps → $1.50.
- Insurance: $600/year = $150/quarter = $50/month — communicate small, collect once.
- Theater ticket: lost $10 ticket → 54 % rebuy; lost $10 cash → 88 % buy (46 % see a $20 mental price).
- Display: "$16.70" > "16.70" > "17" > "seventeen" in pain-center activation (decreasing).
- Upward-sloping demand applies in ~5 % of cases — the exception.

## Worked Example
A telecom operator tested two plan menus with the same respondents. Menu 1: $25 basic and $60 premium — 78 % took the $25 plan, 22 % the $60, ARPU $32.80. Menu 2: same endpoints plus a $50 plan inserted between them. The cheap plan's share collapsed to 44 %, 42 % chose the new $50 plan, 14 % chose $60. ARPU rose to $40.50, +23 %, with zero change in list prices or costs. The middle option absorbed uncertainty about usage, quality doubt about the cheap plan, fear of variable charges, and status motives all at once. Same mechanism in the bank case: a decoy card option nobody bought (2 %) moved combo share from 59 % to 81 % and revenue per customer +28 %.

## Key Takeaways
1. Before touching a premium price, establish whether the demand curve has an upward stretch; if it does, the optimum is above it — climb gradually with a visible upgrade.
2. A price that is "too low" is a sales barrier when buyers cannot judge quality; fix it with a richer, higher-priced package, not a cut.
3. Engineer the anchor: add a top-end option (it need not sell) and design the range so the intended tier is the middle.
4. Three options beat two; a mid-tier or same-price decoy lifts average revenue 20–30 % in the cases shown.
5. Under a threshold, go to .99; if you cannot prove the threshold, test crossing it — thin-margin resellers double profit on the 99¢→$1 step.
6. Frame the price in the smallest credible unit; collect it in the fewest payments — unless continued usage and renewal depend on the customer feeling the payment (fitness).
7. Return value tangibly (cash back, negotiated discount, collectible rewards); take payment intangibly; but keep list prices credible or the anchor dies.
8. Apply all of the above with caution: these are exceptions (~5 %), lab-heavy, and context-bound; most transactions still obey the ordinary downward-sloping demand curve.
