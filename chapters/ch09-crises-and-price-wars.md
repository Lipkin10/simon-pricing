# Chapter 9: Pricing in Crises and Price Wars

## Core Idea
A crisis is a demand collapse that turns the market into a buyer's market; the profit-preserving response is to cut volume and capacity, not price, because a price cut hits profit in full while a volume cut is cushioned by saved variable cost. Price wars are the same mechanism run by competitors, and overcapacity is the root cause of both.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Cut Volume or Cut Price?**: Given the same 5% shock, losing volume costs far less profit than losing price, because the volume loss also removes variable cost.
  - When to use: any crisis decision framed as "accept lower price to keep the plant busy" vs "accept fewer units at the same price".
  - How: (1) compute profit under Alternative A (price −x%, volume constant) and Alternative B (volume −x%, price constant); (2) expect B to win whenever unit contribution is meaningful; (3) treat the managerial pull toward A (utilization, headcount, share) as the bias to resist; (4) act on the factory side first: shorter shifts, plant closures, leaving product unmade (the "one car less than demand" posture).
- **Making Intelligent Price Cuts**: If a cut is unavoidable, structure it to minimize margin damage and maximize the volume response.
  - When to use: after volume/capacity levers are exhausted and a concession is still required.
  - How: (1) accept that the demand curve has shifted down, so the old price-volume relationship no longer holds; (2) assume competitors will match, so share gains are illusory; (3) make the cut highly visible (price advertising, placement, signage) because a cut only works if noticed; (4) make it large and rare rather than small and permanent; (5) check the break-even volume multiple before committing (see Hela).
- **Offer Cash or Goods Instead of Lower Prices!**: Deliver concessions as extra units or services, not as a lower transaction price.
  - When to use: every customer push for a discount during a crisis, in B2B, consumer durables, and leases.
  - How: (1) keep the nominal list price intact; (2) give the "gift" that the customer values at retail but costs you only variable cost; (3) label it explicitly as a temporary crisis measure so it can be withdrawn; (4) prefer free months over lower rent when asset valuation is a rent multiple.
- **Staying Off the Customers' Radar Screen**: Raise prices selectively on the elements customers do not track.
  - When to use: complex price systems (banking fees, large assortments, spare parts, tourism) where only a few key prices are salient.
  - How: (1) inventory every price and product component with transaction counts, assets, returns; (2) survey the customer-facing team for perceived sensitivity per element; (3) raise the low-elasticity, low-visibility items; (4) in spare parts, segment exclusive (OEM-only) parts from commodity parts and price each tier separately.
- **Overcapacity as the Arch-Nemesis**: Persistent price pressure has a root cause, and it is usually excess capacity; price efforts fail until capacity is cut.
  - When to use: an industry where "nobody makes money" despite few players and high concentration.
  - How: (1) diagnose capacity vs demand at industry level; (2) recognise the prisoner's dilemma (cutting alone loses share); (3) use legal signaling (announce intentions, announce that share will be defended, announce retaliation) as a capacity instrument, not only a price instrument; (4) keep announcement and action consistent, and keep the sales force disciplined so the signal is not contradicted in the field.
- **Price Increases in Times of Crisis**: A crisis also reopens the price proposition upward for segments still able and willing to pay.
  - When to use: when a target segment is largely unaffected, or when the whole industry carries the same cost inflation from low utilization.
  - How: pair the increase with a value upgrade (Panera) or with an industry-wide cost justification (stainless steel at 45% utilization).
- **Price War Avoidance and Exit**: Price wars start from overcapacity, commoditization, slow growth, aggressive managers and unrealistic share targets; they end through signaling and value-competition rhetoric.
  - When to use: before any aggressive price move, and while stuck in a war.
  - How: (1) set realistic revenue, volume and share targets; (2) curb aggressive statements and behavior toward competitors, save toughness for customer negotiation; (3) "jawbone" publicly about value competition; (4) before a big cut, announce its purpose and duration so competitors do not read it as an attack; (5) remember one self-destructive competitor is enough to sink the industry.

## Key Concepts
- **Buyer's market**: crisis state where the balance of power has moved to customers; signaled by low utilization, rising inventory, price pressure, and selling pressure.
- **Demand curve shift**: the crisis moves the whole curve down; a price cut does not restore prior volume at the old price.
- **Asymmetric noticeability**: a price increase should go unnoticed, a price cut must be noticed to work.
- **Discount in goods**: a concession whose customer-perceived value is retail price but whose cost to the supplier is variable cost.
- **Temporary crisis measure**: a concession framed as time-bound so the nominal price never has to be "restored".
- **Price transparency gap**: the set of price elements customers neither notice nor recall, which are the safe increase candidates.
- **Prisoner's dilemma of capacity**: unilateral capacity cuts lose share unless competitors follow; same structure as a unilateral price increase.
- **Signaling**: legal public announcement of intentions used to coordinate without colluding, for price and for capacity.
- **Jawboning**: repeated public advocacy of value competition over price competition.
- **Smart vs self-destructive industries**: the former avoid price wars and stay profitable; the latter get stuck in them.

## Mental Models
- Use the "produce one car less than the market wants" posture when fixed costs are high and variable costs are low: the very cost structure that rewards volume in booms is what pushes salespeople to buy volume with price in a bust.
- Think of a discount in goods as arbitrage between two valuations: the customer prices your gift at $100, your P&L prices it at $60; a cash discount forces you to pay the full $100.
- Use "what does the customer actually look at?" before any crisis price move: raise what is invisible, cut (if you must) what is visible and cut it loudly.
- Think of a price cut without an explanation as a message the competitor will write for you, and they will write "attack".

## Anti-patterns
- **Cutting price to protect utilization**: fails because the cut hits profit in full and competitors match, so volume is not saved and the margin is gone for years.
- **Expecting old volume at a new lower price**: fails because the demand curve has shifted and buyers are hoarding cash out of uncertainty, not price resistance.
- **Small permanent discounts**: fail through burnout; a 20% discount on everything, always, stops moving volume.
- **Cutting list price instead of adding goods**: fails on price level, profit, and volume, and is far harder to reverse.
- **Trying to raise prices without touching capacity**: fails while overcapacity remains; it is treating the symptom.
- **Market-share targets detached from reality**: GM's "29" pin drove price aggression and ended in bankruptcy.
- **Silent aggressive price cuts**: a 30% clear-out cut with no stated purpose reads as a share grab and triggers retaliation.

## Numbers & Thresholds
- Power tool base case: price $100, variable cost $60, fixed cost $30M, 1M units, profit $10M.
- Price −5% (volume flat): profit −50% (to $5M). Volume −5% (price flat): profit −20% (to $8M); the gap is $3M because 50,000 fewer units save $3M of variable cost.
- Price and volume both −5%: revenue −9.75%, profit −67.5%. Both −20%: revenue −36%, loss of $14M. Both −30%: revenue −51%.
- 2009 capacity cuts: BASF shut 80 plants; ArcelorMittal cut output by one-third from Nov 2008; Delta cut foreign capacity 15% and domestic 6%; American Airlines 7.5%; Champagne left one-third of grapes unpicked against a 20% demand drop.
- Cash for clunkers (US): $3,500–$4,500 per trade-in against an average residual value of $1,475; initial $1B exhausted in two weeks, extended by $2B. Germany: net discounts over 30%; budget grew from $2B to $7B.
- Hela: 20% off everything on one Sunday; at 25% gross margin it needs 5x normal daily sales to earn the same profit.
- Playground equipment "buy 5 get 6th free" at $10,000: effective cut 16.7%; revenue $50,000, 6 units, contribution $14,000. Flat 16.7% discount instead: price $8,330, revenue $41,650, 5 units, contribution $11,650.
- Discount-in-goods arithmetic: gift worth $100 to the customer costs the supplier $60.
- Regional bank: below-radar increases produced several hundred thousand dollars of revenue with zero complaints.
- Car-maker spare parts: average price increase 12% yielded profit +20% vs base.
- Wind power capacity: 2x global demand (2013). Autos: 80.1M record sales in 2011 vs 100M capacity. Engineering oligopoly: 4 suppliers held 80% of the market and none profited; after one exit and industry-wide cuts, share price went from $13 (2009) to over $100 (2015).
- Panera 2009: raised prices, added a $16.99 lobster sandwich, targeted the 90% still employed; revenue +4%, profit +28%.
- US stainless steel, June 2009: +5–6% price increase at 45% capacity utilization; accepted by nearly all customers.
- Global Pricing Study (2,713 managers, 50 countries): 59% in a price war; Japan 74%, Germany 53%, USA and Belgium 46%. Blame: 82% say a competitor started it, 12% admit deliberate start, 5% admit unintentional start.
- Cloud price war 2014: Amazon, Microsoft, Google cuts of up to 85%.
- GM US share: goal 29% (2002 pin); actual 19.9% in 2009, 17.9% in 2012.
- Anecdote: "$2 billion to drive Competitor X out" was approved instantly, then never executed.

## Worked Example
A playground-equipment maker facing a 2009 demand collapse must give resellers a concession. Option 1, discount in goods: buy five, sixth free. Reseller pays 5 × $10,000 = $50,000 and receives six units, an effective 16.7% cut. With variable cost of $6,000/unit, contribution is $50,000 − 6 × $6,000 = $14,000, and six units flow through the factory. Option 2, the same 16.7% as a flat cut: price becomes $8,330, reseller buys five, revenue $41,650, contribution $41,650 − 5 × $6,000 = $11,650. Option 1 wins on nominal price (still $10,000), contribution (+$2,350), and volume (+1 unit), and because it is labelled a crisis measure it simply expires; option 2 would require dragging list price from $8,330 back to $10,000 later.

## Key Takeaways
1. In a crisis, choose the volume loss over the price loss; run the A/B profit arithmetic before any concession.
2. Attack the factory first: cut shifts, close lines, leave product unmade; the cost structure that rewards volume in booms punishes it in a bust.
3. If a cut is unavoidable, make it big, rare, loud, and time-boxed; check the volume multiple needed to break even.
4. Give goods, services, or free months, never a lower nominal price; the customer values it at retail, you pay variable cost, and it withdraws itself.
5. Raise the prices nobody watches: inventory every price element, survey your front line for sensitivity, and lift the low-elasticity items.
6. If the industry cannot make money, the problem is capacity, not price; use legal signaling to lead the capacity cut and keep the sales force from undercutting the message.
7. Prevent price wars with realistic share targets, non-aggressive rhetoric toward competitors, and an explicit public rationale for any large temporary cut.
