# Chapter 2: Everything Revolves Around Price

## Core Idea
Every unit of revenue and profit is the result of a price decision, and the only driver of what a customer will pay is the value that customer perceives. Price and value are one word ("pretium").

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Price = Value (three tasks of management)**: willingness to pay tracks perceived value, so the manager's job splits into create value, communicate value, retain value.
  - When to use: at product conception, not at launch; and whenever a price seems "stuck".
  - How: (1) Create: materials, performance, design, innovation. (2) Communicate: description, selling proposition, brand, packaging, placement. (3) Retain: post-purchase experience and expectations of how long the value lasts (decisive for durables, cars, luxury).
- **Second-order effects and intangible benefits**: value the customer will pay for often sits one step away from the spec sheet (first order) in outcomes they can measure (second order) or feel (intangible).
  - When to use: B2B value communication; any product whose spec-sheet benefits do not move the buyer.
  - How: commission or gather hard data on the downstream outcome (fewer accidents, fewer sick days, energy savings over the asset's life), express it in money, and bring that number to the negotiation. For consumer goods, use a durable true claim (Miele: machines last 20 years).
- **Two-dimensional price system (BahnCard type)**: split the price into a fixed component (annual card fee) and a variable component (discounted ticket).
  - When to use: when the customer's mental comparison uses only variable out-of-pocket cost against a rival, and an outright price cut is impossible.
  - How: (1) find the rival's perceived variable cost; (2) set the discounted variable price below it; (3) charge an upfront fee that recovers margin; (4) offer tiers so the effective discount rises with usage; (5) check the heavy-user cannibalization and antitrust risk before launch.
- **Pricing power**: the ability to achieve your desired prices; its flipside is buying power.
  - When to use: diagnosing whether a business can raise prices without a "prayer session" (Buffett).
  - How: involve top management in setting the pricing framework and run a dedicated pricing department (see numbers below).

## Key Concepts
- **Price**: the number of monetary units paid for a good, but in practice a multi-dimensional structure (base price, discounts, package/segment/time/location differentiation, complements, two-part tariffs, bundles, negotiated and channel prices).
- **Price aliases**: premium, fee, honorarium, tuition, toll, rent, commission, salary, schedule of charges; price is price whatever the label.
- **Value to customer**: the subjective, perceived value; objective value or labor input matters only insofar as the customer believes it does.
- **Out-of-pocket cost**: the variable cost a customer notices daily (gasoline), ignoring fixed costs (insurance, depreciation).
- **Effective discount**: the real average saving after the upfront fee is counted, versus the headline discount the customer perceives.
- **Market-clearing price**: the intersection of the upward supply curve and downward demand curve; the only equilibrium price.
- **Hog cycle (boom-and-bust cycle)**: delayed supply response to price causes oscillation; 10 to 15 years in oil exploration.
- **Cartel**: explicit or tacit agreement to divide markets by price, conditions or volume; the target of antitrust fines in the billions.
- **Pricing creep**: markets and prices entering domains that were free or taboo (boarding priority, visas, surrogacy, immigration rights).

## Mental Models
- Think of price as the split of a pool of money between supplier and customer: a power struggle that ends in a truce, not a zero-sum game.
- Use "le prix s'oublie, la qualité reste" when tempted by the lowest bid: the price is forgotten, the quality endures; better cheated on price than on quality.
- Think of value of advice as unrelated to time spent: 30 minutes for the optimal answer beats three days for a sub-optimal one.
- Use the hog cycle as the default expectation for commodity prices: cycles are far more likely than sustained trends.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reflexive discounting**: promotions rose from 50% to 70% of beer sales in Germany with discounts up to 50%; Best Buy's 2013 holiday promotions did not raise demand, they only cut the price of what would have sold anyway, and the stock fell almost 30% in a day.
- **Retreating to cost management out of price fear**: internal and supplier levers feel safer, but they abandon the lever that drives every dollar of revenue.
- **"A good product sells itself"**: value is necessary, not sufficient; the carmaker board member who believed this in the mid-1980s runs a company now in trouble.
- **Copying another industry's price system without the customer**: DB's 2002 airline-style advance-booking system killed the BahnCard 50 and triggered a near consumer revolt; the managers were fired.
- **Government price setting by political rather than market signals**: produces oversupply (butter mountains) or undersupply (rent control).
- **Bundling attractive with unattractive events**: London 2012 refused it and refused all discounts even for unsold events, protecting the value signal.

## Numbers & Thresholds
- Beer promotions in Germany: 50% of sales on promotion, then 70% two years later, discounts up to 50%.
- Best Buy shares: down almost 30% the day after 2013 holiday results.
- Simon-Kucher Global Pricing Study (2012, 2,700+ managers, 50 countries): only 33% report high pricing power; pricing power 35% higher when top management sets the pricing framework; 24% higher with a dedicated pricing department.
- London 2012 Olympics: lowest price £20.12, highest £2,012; "Pay Your Age" for under-18s (£6 for a six-year-old, £16 for a sixteen-year-old); no discounts, no event bundles, public transport bundled in; 99% sold online; target £376M ($625M), actual £660M ($1.1B), 75% above target and more than Beijing, Athens and Sydney combined.
- BahnCard 1992: rail ~16 US cents/km vs car gasoline ~10 cents/km (500 km: $80 vs $50); card ~$140/yr second class, $280 first class, half price for seniors and students; 50% ticket discount cut variable cost to 8 cents/km; 1M cards in four months; 4M by 2000.
- BahnCard 2003 relaunch: project started two days after 18 May 2003, announced 2 July, reintroduced 1 August (six weeks); BahnCard 25, 50 and 100 (100% discount) tiers.
- BahnCard today: ~5M holders; fees from 61 EUR (~$80, BahnCard 25 second class) to $8,900 (BahnCard 100 first class); BahnCard 50 at 249 EUR (~$325) second class, 498 EUR (~$650) first class; BahnCard 100 holders 42,000 vs under 1,000 network passes per year before.
- BahnCard 50 effective discount: just under 30% average actual saving while customers perceive 50% on every ticket.
- Airline "Fly & Save" card: ~$7,000, shelved for antitrust risk and heavy-user cannibalization.
- GE 2012 annual report: energy-savings value expressed in dollars over 15 years.
- Miele: claims 20-year life, mother's 1964 machine lasted almost 40 years (to 2003); repeat purchase rate near 100% despite high prices.
- Tax advisor: $1,500 for 30 minutes (15 to understand, 15 to solve) vs three days from an ordinary consultant.
- Reaping machine: $600 bargain, unreliable, scrapped after two years.
- Oil hog cycle: ~$20/barrel in 1997, forecasts ~$15, actual $12 by early 1999, peak $147.90 in July 2008 (more than 10x 1999), below $50 in 2015; exploration-to-production lag ~10 years, cycles 10 to 15 years.
- Antitrust fines: $1.9B (seven tube makers, Dec 2012); $2.3B (six banks, derivatives, Dec 2013); ~$1.2B (St. Gobain, auto glass, 2008); over $1B and 12 managers jailed (US auto parts).
- Pricing creep: EasyJet priority boarding $16; US ESTA entry $14; concierge doctor $1,500/yr; mercenaries $250 to $1,000/day; surrogacy in India $6,250; US immigration right $500,000.

## Worked Example
Situation (1991): Deutsche Bahn losing passengers to cars; a second-class ticket cost ~16 cents/km while drivers compared it only with gasoline at ~10 cents/km, so a 500 km trip read as $80 by rail against $50 by car. Cutting fares below 10 cents/km was not viable. Decision: mirror the car's cost structure the customer ignores. Split the rail price into a fixed annual card (~$140 second class, $280 first class, half for seniors and students) plus a 50% ticket discount, taking the noticed variable cost to 8 cents/km, under the car's 10. Outcome: over 1M cards in four months, 4M by 2000, ~5M today; BahnCard holders generate the lion's share of long-distance revenue. The 2003 analysis showed the average real saving was just under 30% while customers perceived 50%, so DB bought a 50% perception for under 30% of margin. Killing the card in 2002 for an airline-style system caused a revolt and a six-week emergency relaunch with 25/50/100 tiers.

## Key Takeaways
1. Price is the only lever that touches every dollar of revenue; fear of it pushes managers to cost-cutting that cannot compensate.
2. Start pricing at product conception and run the three tasks: create, communicate, retain value.
3. In B2B, translate second-order outcomes into money over the asset's life; in B2C, use one true durable claim.
4. When the customer compares only variable cost, use a fixed-plus-variable structure to win the comparison without cutting the headline price; make the effective discount lower than the perceived one.
5. Before launching a two-part tariff, model heavy-user cannibalization, break-even points per tier, and antitrust exposure.
6. Hold firm on no-discount, no-bundle policies when the goal is to signal value (London 2012); use price digits themselves as communication.
7. Put top management on the pricing framework and staff a pricing department: 35% and 24% more pricing power respectively.
8. Expect commodity prices to cycle, and read a rising price as a signal that supply is about to grow.
