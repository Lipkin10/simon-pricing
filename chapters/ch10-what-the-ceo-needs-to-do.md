# Chapter 10: What the CEO Needs to Do

## Core Idea
Price is the strongest profit driver, profit drives market capitalization, so pricing belongs on the CEO's desk — and the CEO's first job is avoiding catastrophic price moves, because bad price decisions hit the share price immediately while good ones pay off slowly.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Strict profit orientation** (the author's answer to "what's the silver bullet?"): no silver bullet exists; run the company on profit, not share.
  - When to use: mature markets where a market-share obsession has become culture.
  - How: (1) repeat the profit mantra daily; (2) tie country/unit manager incentives to profit only — not revenue, volume or share; (3) never start a price war and don't answer every competitor move; (4) where you lead the market, take price leadership via a sustained price-and-value communication campaign; (5) stop punishing share loss when it buys profit (Poland subsidiary: ended a price war, raised prices, leader followed, profit up, slight share loss — first time the CEO did not criticize).
- **Three prerequisites of good pricing**: create value, quantify value, communicate value — then you get "the price you deserve".
- **Price → market capitalization chain**: price increase → after-tax profit → × PE ratio = market cap. Assume the increase sticks and the PE holds constant; multiply the incremental profit by the PE.
  - Use to translate any pricing program into an enterprise-value number for the board or a PE buyer.
- **Asymmetry of price decisions**: a poor price move destroys share price at once; a sound one shows up as a modest, delayed, steady climb. Implication: avoiding serious pricing mistakes matters more than finding the perfect strategy. Two mistake classes to avoid: sudden short-term shocks (Marlboro, J.C. Penney, Abercrombie) and wrong long-term positioning (Praktiker).
- **Key pricing indicators / relative price**: own price expressed relative to the average price of the relevant competitive models (Toyota's system). Compute at product, product-group, business-unit, country and company level so top management can judge the price position and its drift.
- **Incentive rule**: price-based incentives for salespeople, yes; for top managers, no — reward shareholder-value growth, not the individual instrument (price) used to reach it.
- **Relative value of growth (RVG)** (Nathaniel Mass, HBR 2005): how much 1% revenue growth adds to shareholder value relative to 1% margin improvement; RVG = 2 means revenue growth is worth twice a margin point. The author's critique: it assumes growth is pure volume; price growth and volume growth must be split, and price growth is far more profit-rich (Fig. 5.3: pure price growth +50% profit vs pure volume growth +20%).

### What a CEO must do about price (the chapter's directives, as a checklist)
- [ ] Lead with a long-term profit orientation; kill the share-loss stigma in the culture.
- [ ] Set manager incentives on profit; set sales incentives on price realization; set top-management incentives on shareholder value, not price per se.
- [ ] Never start a price war; don't reflexively match aggressive competitors.
- [ ] Where you are market leader, exercise price leadership and communicate value publicly.
- [ ] Know the fundamentals: price gaps to competitors, gaps across countries, floor-price processes, results — not every price, but the facts, processes and outcomes.
- [ ] Install key pricing indicators (relative prices) at every aggregation level and track them.
- [ ] Audit process rules for perverse effects (the 20% floor that became the ceiling).
- [ ] Stay out of routine customer negotiations — a CEO in the room is where concessions leak.
- [ ] Consider a Chief Pricing Officer per division reporting to the division head (GE, 2001).
- [ ] Test radical price-model changes before rolling them out (J.C. Penney did not).
- [ ] Never build the entire positioning on a discount slogan; once committed, exit is nearly impossible (Praktiker).
- [ ] Don't get arrogant after a run of success (Netflix).
- [ ] Have investor relations communicate pricing power and price discipline to analysts.

## Key Concepts
- **PE ratio**: market cap ÷ after-tax profit; DJIA average 16.6 (May 16, 2014), roughly the long-term norm.
- **Pricing power**: Buffett's single most important criterion in evaluating a business; analysts historically ignore it because financial statements carry no price data.
- **Price war**: the move that transfers value from every player to customers; the CEO's job is not to start one.
- **Price leadership**: the leader signalling and holding price levels so the industry follows (telecom case; Poland case).
- **Price discipline**: strict adherence of the sales force to set prices/floors; a public program of it doubled a telecom's share price in six months.
- **Praktiker syndrome** (Dieter Schindel, Woolworth): a chain defined by permanent discount claims that cannot escape them; Woolworth's answer was permanent cuts on 400+ items instead of discount campaigns.
- **Floor price**: the internal minimum a deal is accepted at; in practice it becomes the price nearly every deal closes at.
- **Chief Pricing Officer**: GE's divisional pricing role (2001) that tightened discipline, raised target-price attainment and trained negotiators.
- **Hidden Champions**: little-known global leaders priced 10–15% above market, with returns 2.4× the industry average, and CEOs deep in price decisions.

## Mental Models
- Think of a 2% price increase as a market-cap event, not a P&L line: profit uplift × PE ≈ +26.7% enterprise value on average.
- Use "which mistake am I about to make?" before "which strategy is optimal?" — the downside is immediate and asymmetric.
- Think of any approval threshold as the number sales will converge on; a floor becomes a target.
- Think of the CEO's calendar as a pricing instrument: presence in customer meetings is a discount channel.

## Anti-patterns
- **Cutting price to fight no-names from a strong brand (Marlboro Friday, Philip Morris, 2 April 1993)**: investors read it as surrender; −26% share price, −$13bn cap in one day, dragged Coca-Cola and RJR Nabisco, DJIA −2%.
- **Building identity on a permanent discount slogan (Praktiker)**: "20% off everything" then "25% off anything with a plug"; share price $40+ (2007) → <$13 (2008) → ~$1.90 (2013) → bankruptcy 2013, while the category grew $1.3bn to $24.7bn.
- **Breaking an oligopoly's price peace for volume (Uralkali, July 2013)**: +30% output, cheaper China pricing, exit from joint sales; Uralkali −24% in 2 days, Potash Corp −23%, K+S −30%; new floor $305/t vs ~$400 before.
- **A 60% increase on the back of success, justified by your own costs (Netflix, 12 July 2011)**: customers churned modestly, investors cut the stock ~75% in 3 months; cap from >$16bn to <$5bn; suppliers cancelled licences.
- **Eliminating promotions and trading up without testing (J.C. Penney, 1 Feb 2012)**: revenue −3%, profit $378m → loss $152m; share $41.81 → $14.67 (−65%) while DJIA +16%; CEO fired April 2013.
- **Discount campaign into rising unit costs (Abercrombie & Fitch, Q3 2011)**: revenue up, gross margin crushed, analyst downgrade, share price −30%+.
- **One-sided approval thresholds (engineering CEO)**: "below 20% margin needs my sign-off" turned 24–25% deals into permanent 20.1%.
- **Floors nobody supervises (automotive supplier)**: nearly every contract won landed exactly at the floor; the CEO didn't know the floor existed.
- **CEO annual visits to customer CEOs (logistics provider)**: each visit extracted concessions and undid months of sales work; stopping them improved margins.

## Numbers & Thresholds
- DJIA PE 16.6 (16 May 2014); selected Fig. 10.1 companies PE 17.93.
- +2% price, constant PE → Sony +$42.73bn cap; sample average +$48.88bn = +26.7% on $182.8bn average cap.
- Parking-garage PE deal: differentiated garage-level increases locked +$10m/yr into lessor contracts; sold at PE 12 → +$120m enterprise value.
- Marlboro Friday: −26% share, −$13bn, DJIA −2%; first fall in US ad spend since 1970.
- Praktiker: ~25,000 staff, ~$5bn revenue; share $40 → $13 → $1.90.
- Potash: ~$400/t stable → analyst floor $288/t (≈K+S cost) → China deal $305/t, ~25% below H1 2013; K+S profit forecast cut 84%.
- Netflix: 25m customers (summer 2011); +60% price; −75% share in 3 months; cap >$16bn → <$5bn ($5.5bn in 2014).
- J.C. Penney: ~75% of merchandise sold at ≥50% off pre-Johnson; 100+ boutiques; −65% share vs DJIA +16%; <$10 by 2015.
- Abercrombie: −30%+ share; ~$20 in 2015.
- Telecom: −67% share over 2 years before discipline program; doubled within 6 months after.
- Credit Suisse (Oct 2010): +1 percentage point price → +16% DCF fair value.
- Fig. 5.3 recall: pure price growth +50% profit; pure volume growth +20%.
- GE under Welch (1982–2001): profit ×7; share 53¢ → $27.95 = +5,273%.
- Porsche: operating return on sales 18.0% (2013) vs 17.5% (2012).
- Hidden Champions: prices 10–15% above market; returns 2.4× industry.
- Global Pricing Study 2012 (2,713 managers, 50+ countries), strong CEO involvement: pricing power +35%; price-increase success rate +18%; 26% more achieved higher margins post-increase; 30% had a pricing department. Strong pricing power → +25% returns.
- Engineering rule: <20% gross margin needs CEO approval → deals settle at 20.1%.

## Worked Example
Parking-garage operator, PE-owned ~5 years. Costs already cut, garages already added — the conventional levers were spent, price untouched. Analysis found room mostly in large cities. Instead of a flat increase, each garage got its own increase according to attractiveness, utilization and local competition. The new prices were written into the lessor contracts, securing +$10m/year. Sale a few months later at PE 12: $10m × 12 = $120m added to the exit price. Rule to reuse: contractually lock the price gain before valuation, and price it per unit, not across the board.

## Key Takeaways
1. Run on profit; make share loss acceptable when it buys margin, and say so publicly to your managers.
2. Convert every pricing program into market-cap terms (Δprofit × PE) before presenting it — that is the number owners react to.
3. Prioritize not losing: no unforced price cuts from strength, no positioning built on discount slogans, no untested radical price-model swaps.
4. Price growth beats volume growth for profit; ask analysts and reports to separate the two.
5. Know your floor-price process, your relative prices and your approval thresholds — each silently sets where deals land.
6. Incentivize sales on price, executives on shareholder value; keep the CEO out of routine negotiations.
7. In due diligence and post-acquisition, treat untapped price potential as a value lever equal to cost cuts — it needs a series of small moves over years, not one leap.
