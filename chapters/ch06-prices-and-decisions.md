# Chapter 6: Prices and Decisions

## Core Idea
Every path to profit starts at price, and the only way to find the profit-maximizing price is to quantify the demand curve (price-response function) and the competitors' likely reaction — cost-plus and competitor-following are shortcuts that land you too high or too low by luck.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The interrelationships of price management (Fig. 6.1)**: price reaches profit through three direct paths (Price → Revenue → Profit; Price → Volume → Revenue → Profit; Price → Volume → Costs → Profit) plus four more once competition, time and resellers enter (Price → Competitors' prices → Market share → Volume; Price today → Volume/Costs future; Supplier price → Reseller price → Volume).
  - When to use: before any price change larger than a small increment; small changes tolerate the "only price moves" assumption, large ones do not.
  - How: trace each path with numbers (price, cost, volume are the three inputs) instead of experience or rules of thumb.

- **Cost-plus pricing**: cost × standard markup (e.g., $5 cost, 100% markup → $10).
  - When to use: as a floor check only. Pros the author concedes: hard data, guaranteed positive unit contribution, and a de facto price cartel if competitors with similar buying power do the same.
  - Fails because: it ignores willingness to pay; customers may only pay $10 in small numbers (market choked) or would pay $12 (margin sacrificed).

- **Following the competition**: match, or price a fixed % above/below a reference competitor (safety-products firm: 10% below the premium supplier; a major retailer: 600 top SKUs = >50% of revenue pegged to Aldi).
  - Fails because: you outsource your pricing department to a rival with a different cost structure and demand pattern.

- **Market-based price setting**: set price from the demand curve, costs and goals together.
  - How: (1) get a demand curve, (2) tabulate revenue, variable cost, contribution, profit across a price range (Fig. 6.2), (3) pick the profit maximum, (4) check sensitivity left and right.

- **Sharing Value Fifty-Fifty (linear demand)**: Optimal price = ½ × (maximum price + variable unit cost), where maximum price = the price at which volume hits zero.
  - How: pass on half of any variable-cost change, half of any exchange-rate or tax shock, half of any gain in willingness to pay; give the customer half of a value advantage.
  - Elasticity conditions: near the profit-maximizing price, elasticity must exceed 1; at the revenue-maximizing price, elasticity equals exactly 1. (The author does not name Amoroso-Robinson here; the linear midpoint rule is the formula he commits to.)

- **Five ways to derive a demand curve / price elasticity** (author's recommendation: always combine at least two, cross-check, act when they converge):
  1. **Expert judgment ("one up, one down")**: ask insiders "how much volume do we lose at +10%?" (answer 50% → elasticity 5). Extend to multiple price steps; follow each number with "Why?" and "What happens next?" (competitor reaction); collect written answers individually before consensus; best for new products. Con: no customer input.
  2. **Asking customers directly** (incl. Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter): fast, high volume. Con: makes respondents price-fixated and prestige-biased; never use alone.
  3. **Asking indirectly — conjoint measurement**: respondents trade off bundles of brand, quality, performance and price; adaptive computer versions tighten trade-offs and simulate shopping. The author's most valid survey method.
  4. **Price tests / field experiments**: change real shelf or online prices systematically and track response; now cheap thanks to scanner data and e-commerce.
  5. **Historical market data (econometrics)**: usually useless (Telser 1962). High-elasticity markets show too little price variation; low-elasticity markets show too little volume variation; structural breaks (new entrant, generics, digital, new channel, new product) make history irrelevant.

- **Cross-price elasticity**: % change in own volume ÷ % change in competitor's price (competitor cuts 10%, you lose 6% → 0.6). Positive sign, usually smaller than own-price elasticity; converges toward it as differentiation shrinks. Build competitor price into the demand curve as price difference, relative price, or an extra variable.

- **Prisoner's dilemma in oligopoly**: your outcome depends on whether the rival follows. A cut that gets matched buys volume nowhere and destroys margin; an increase that is not followed costs share and often gets rescinded (the brewery case).

- **Chamberlin vs. Cournot hypotheses (Fig. 6.3)**: Chamberlin = both assume and execute full following (monopoly-like price, price-leadership outcome, Stigler's recommendation for competitive oligopolies); Cournot = both assume no reaction, both react anyway, prices fall.

- **Price leadership**: one player moves first, others follow (GM at ~50% share raising annually; Aldi on milk; AB InBev with MillerCoors following). Breaks when an entrant refuses to follow (Modelo, until absorbed in 2013).

- **Signaling**: announce a planned move publicly to all market participants (customers, investors, regulators included) and listen for return signals; never phrase anything conditional on a competitor ("if X raises, we follow"). Retaliation signals also work (Hyundai on Japanese incentives). Clear every use with legal counsel.

- **Pricing under inflation**: raise at least at the inflation rate, in a series of small regular steps started as early as inflation looms; do not try to buy share with relative price cuts, they get lost in the noise.

## Key Concepts
- **Price-response function**: the demand curve — the equation mapping a price to the units sold.
- **Reservation price / maximum price**: the price equal to perceived value; the customer's willingness to pay in a yes-no purchase.
- **Yes-no vs. variable-quantity demand**: durables are one-unit decisions summed across customers; nondurables are repeated yes-no decisions with declining marginal utility per unit.
- **Nonlinear pricing**: price per unit varies with quantity bought, the seller's option in the variable-quantity case.
- **Price elasticity**: % change in volume ÷ % change in price, sign dropped; not constant along a linear curve.
- **Symmetrical oligopoly**: few equally strong sellers whose demand curves depend on each other's prices.
- **Cantillon effect**: those who receive new money first buy before prices rise; latecomers lose.
- **Sophistication vs. professionalism**: airlines run advanced systems yet still start price wars — tooling is not discipline.

## Mental Models
- Think of a "commodity" as a product no one has bothered to differentiate yet; brand, packaging or service (Evian) creates price leeway.
- Use the midpoint rule when you face any shock (cost, currency, tax, value): move price half the distance, never the full distance.
- Think of pricing in an oligopoly as a staring contest: rigid prices are rational when rivals match cuts but ignore increases.
- Use inflation as a test of signal clarity: if customers cannot read your relative price, the relative price does not exist.

## Anti-patterns
- **Passing cost changes through in full**: cost-plus logic; the optimum absorbs half.
- **Capturing 100% of a value advantage in price**: 20% more value at 20% higher price leaves the customer nothing; take 10%.
- **Pegging your prices to a rival's**: you inherit their cost structure and demand assumptions.
- **Maximizing revenue**: the power-tool revenue peak ($75) is a $7.5 million loss.
- **Believing "err high" is free**: profit loss is symmetrical around the optimum; the only asymmetry is that retreating from a high price is easier than raising a low one.
- **Trusting historical market data**: ~100 of 5,000+ Simon-Kucher projects used econometrics as the key method.
- **Cutting relative price during high inflation**: the Brazilian pain-reliever case lost profit and gained no share.
- **Signaling with conditional language**: anything that reads as an agreement is illegal.

## Numbers & Thresholds
- Power tool: Volume = 3,000,000 − 20,000 × Price; fixed cost $30M; variable cost $60; pmax = 3,000,000 / 20,000 = $150; optimum = ½ (150 + 60) = $105; profit $10.5M.
- $5 off the optimum (either side) → profit −4.8%; $15 off → −42.9%; $90 and $120 yield identical profit.
- Elasticity at $100 = 2 (1% price → 2% volume; +5% price → −10% volume).
- Revenue max at $75: 1.5M units, $112.5M revenue, −$7.5M profit.
- Variable cost $60 → $70: price $105 → $110, not $115; $60 → $50: price → $100.
- Willingness to pay +$10 (pmax $160) → price +$5.
- Sales tax +1% → price rises by less than 1%, exact amount from the slope.
- 20% value advantage → 10% price premium.
- Expert judgment: +10% price / −50% volume → elasticity 5.
- Cross-price elasticity example: −10% competitor price, −6% own volume → 0.6.
- Duopoly: Own volume = 1,000 − 50 × own price + 25 × competitor price; variable cost $5; fixed $5,000; at $20 each earns $2,500; Chamberlin at $22.50 → $2,650 (+6%); Cournot at $16.67 → $1,803 (−27.9%).
- Price leadership: GM at ~50% share; Aldi retailer case 600 SKUs > 50% of revenue.
- German car insurance signaling (Allianz, 2011): prices +7% in 2012, rose again in 2013.
- US CPI 1991–2013: +71.2%, 2.47% average annual inflation; dollar purchasing power −41.6% since 1991, −82.6% since 1971, −96.9% versus gold (37.1 oz per $1,142 in 1971 vs. 1 oz on 1 Sep 2015). "Modest" inflation = ~2%/year.
- Brazil 1980s: inflation of several hundred % per year.

## Worked Example
Power tool division: Volume = 3,000,000 − 20,000 × P, fixed $30M, variable $60/unit. At P = $100 you sell 1,000,000 units, revenue $100M, contribution $40M, profit $10M. At P = $105 volume drops to 900,000, revenue $94.5M, variable cost $54M, contribution $40.5M, profit $10.5M — the maximum. Shortcut: pmax = $150 (where volume = 0), so optimum = ½ (150 + 60) = $105. Miss by $5 either way and profit falls to $10M (−4.8%); miss by $15 ($90 or $120) and profit falls to $6M (−42.9%), identical on both sides. Push for revenue at $75 and you lose $7.5M. Raise variable cost to $70 and the new optimum is $110: the customer absorbs $5, you absorb $5.

## Key Takeaways
1. Never set a price without a numeric demand curve; combine expert judgment + conjoint at minimum, and act when the methods converge.
2. For linear demand, price at the midpoint of maximum price and variable cost; pass on exactly half of any cost, currency, tax or value change.
3. Being near the optimum matters more than nailing it: ±$5 costs 4.8%, ±$15 costs 42.9% — the loss accelerates with distance.
4. Elasticity near the optimum must exceed 1; if insiders say a 10% cut adds 50% volume, a cut is on the table; if a 10% increase loses 50%, it is not.
5. In an oligopoly, ask "what happens next?" before every move; if rivals match cuts but not increases, hold price (input-cost increases excepted, since they hit everyone).
6. Prefer price leadership and lawful public signaling to guessing; get legal sign-off on every signal.
7. Under inflation, raise at least with inflation, in small regular steps starting early; never try to buy share on relative price.
