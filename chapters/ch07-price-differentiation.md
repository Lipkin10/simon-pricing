# Chapter 7: Price Differentiation: The High Art

## Core Idea
One optimal uniform price captures only about half of the profit available in a market; the rest sits with buyers who would pay more and buyers priced out above variable cost. Charging different prices to different buyers, units, times, places or groups is how you reach the rest — but only if you can fence the segments and only up to the point where the cost of finer differentiation exceeds its shrinking gain.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Profit rectangle → profit triangle**: uniform price = rectangle inside the demand triangle; with linear demand and linear cost the triangle is exactly 2× the rectangle.
  - When to use: as the first diagnostic on any one-price business — the untapped area is the upper bound on what differentiation can add.
  - How: (1) locate the two forgone areas — high-WTP buyers paying less than they would, and buyers between price and variable cost who don't buy; (2) pick a differentiation lever per area; (3) accept that the full triangle is unreachable — you would need every buyer's individual maximum and perfect separation.
- **Two-price differentiation (yes-no purchase)**: split the demand curve into a high price and a low price with a fence between them.
  - How: each buyer wants one unit; aggregate individual maximum prices; set the high price for the top block, the low price for the next block; verify the fence before launch (see Anti-patterns).
- **Nonlinear pricing (variable-quantity case)**: a distinct price per unit consumed, following diminishing marginal utility (first unit dearest).
  - When to use: same buyer buys 1..n units or visits; you can track per-customer usage (card, account) so arbitrage is blocked.
  - How: for the 1st unit, find the profit-maximizing price across segments; repeat for the 2nd, 3rd... unit; the resulting descending schedule is the price structure.
- **Price bundling** (pure and mixed): several products at a total below the sum of parts; excess WTP for one item transfers to the other, so bundle-level WTP is more homogeneous and easier to split into buyers/non-buyers.
  - How: assume bundle WTP = sum of item WTPs; compute optimal stand-alone prices; test a bundle price; then test mixed bundling (bundle plus stand-alone items, re-optimizing stand-alone prices).
- **Unbundling**: break a bundle into components. Consider it when (a) components have low price elasticity and the bundle price has drifted high; (b) stand-alone sale opens new segments; (c) components are becoming standardized/compatible (pure bundling gets riskier as the market matures); (d) the value chain is shifting toward charging separately for services once included.
- **Multi-person pricing**: price set for a group, varying with headcount; same two effects as bundling (better surplus capture, WTP transfer between persons).
- **Full-volume vs incremental volume discount**: same rate table, different base. Seller chooses incremental; buyer asks for full-volume.
- **Person-specific differentiation**: by qualifying class (student, senior, club card), by cost/risk (credit behavior), by inferred price sensitivity (device, time of day). Accepted forms are class-based; profile-based pricing risks backlash.
- **Location-based differentiation and the INTERPRICE model**: distance is the natural fence; across countries, set optimal price corridors that exploit purchasing-power gaps while keeping gray imports tolerable — never one flat international price.
- **Time-based differentiation / dynamic pricing / revenue (yield) management**: allocate capacity to price points from a demand forecast and re-allocate as bookings arrive. The decision variable is price elasticity per period, not demand level.
- **Hi-Lo vs EDLP**: alternate regular and promotional prices vs constant low prices. No clear winner in the evidence; choose against what relevant competitors do and by customer income mix.
- **Advance-sale / early-bird prices**: an effective fence for flights (price-sensitive leisure books early, business late); weak for events and tours, where the real motive is early cash and the cost is forgone late high-price sales.
- **Penetration vs skimming** (new-product entry): low launch price to seed adoption when word of mouth, experience curve or scale effects are strong and the product is an experience good; high launch price stepping down over time when the price signals quality/prestige, capacity is limited, or costs will fall with volume. Skimming is complemented by **versioning** (constant price, rising performance each generation).
- **Fencing**: separation of segments so high-WTP buyers cannot buy at the low price. Works when the value gap between tiers is large enough and access is controlled; requires more than price — versioning, channels, access control, targeted messages, language.
- **Information and profit cliffs**: differentiation needs WTP at individual or segment level (and per-unit marginal utility for nonlinear pricing); overshooting by a little, from thin data, drops profit off a cliff.
- **Optimal degree of differentiation**: profit gain per added price step falls while information/control cost rises disproportionately; stop where value minus cost peaks, never at maximum granularity.

## Key Concepts
- **Consumer surplus**: gap between a buyer's maximum price and what they actually pay.
- **Yes-no purchase**: each buyer takes at most one unit, so the demand curve is the sorted list of individual maximum prices.
- **Variable-quantity case**: quantity bought depends on price; governed by diminishing marginal utility.
- **Vickrey (second-price) auction**: mechanism where revealing true WTP is the bidder's best move; one of the few ways to observe WTP directly.
- **Arbitrage / gray (parallel) imports**: resale from low-price to high-price segment or country; appears when price gaps are large and arbitrage costs are low.
- **Perishable capacity**: rooms, seats, fresh goods whose cost is sunk at the last minute; any price above zero beats spoilage short-term, but predictable last-minute pricing trains buyers and cannibalizes full-price sales.
- **Peak-load pricing**: differentiation aimed at balancing supply and demand; dynamic pricing adds a profit motive.
- **Price gouging**: raising prices under scarcity or emergency; economically rationing, socially resisted and banned in some jurisdictions.
- **Experience goods**: value understood only after use; candidates for penetration or freemium entry.
- **Hard vs soft data**: unsold capacity is hard and pushes price down; untapped WTP is soft and uncertain — revenue managers systematically over-weight the hard side.

## Mental Models
- Think of a uniform price as leaving roughly half the market's profit on the table; the largest pricing gains come from structure, not from fine-tuning one number.
- Use a differentiated price only when you can name the fence; without it, expect the two-price outcome to fall below the one-price outcome.
- Think of a bundle as a transfer of surplus WTP between items; the more heterogeneous the item-level WTPs, the more a bundle homogenizes them and the more it pays.
- Use price elasticity per period, not demand level, to decide off-peak discounts: an empty Sunday is not evidence that the price is too high.
- Think of the cheapest tier as deliberately degraded (Dupuit's roofless third class): the low tier's job is to keep the rich from buying it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Differentiating without a fence**: if high-WTP buyers migrate to the low price, profit falls below the uniform-price result (power-tool case: −43%).
- **Discounting low-demand periods**: the garage at 1 EUR on Sundays stays empty; the cinema's 25% weekday discounts moved nothing. Low demand was mistaken for high elasticity.
- **Predictable last-minute pricing**: customers learn to wait; fence collapses; many firms rightly let goods spoil instead.
- **Blanket uniform international pricing**: after EU unification, firms threw away corridor profits; the answer is corridors, not one price.
- **Full-volume discounts as seller**: halves profit relative to incremental at the same rate table.
- **Profile-based price discrimination made visible**: Amazon's DVD pricing by profile triggered outcry and refunds; Coca-Cola's temperature-priced vending machines were withdrawn after protests.
- **Launching too low under a "penetration" label**: sold-out-on-day-one (Bayern Munich season, Audi Q7 80,000 orders vs 70,000 capacity, Playmobil Ark reselling above launch price, HP Series 4 hitting the annual target in a month, Asus eee meeting 10% of demand) means price was wrong, not clever.
- **Dressing up a rescue cut as skimming**: Nokia Lumia 900 $99 → $49.99 after three months, with the share price down 64%.
- **Skimming without demand analysis**: Sanofi Zaltrap at $11,063/month refused by Sloan-Kettering, price halved within months.
- **Trusting transaction data as WTP data**: online records show what was paid, not the sensitivity behind it; Big Data does not substitute for measured WTP.
- **Maximum granularity**: profit steps flatten while cost of control steepens; chasing the whole triangle is a net loss.

## Numbers & Thresholds
- Uniform price captures ~50% of the profit triangle (exactly 50% with linear demand and linear cost); nonlinear demand moves it somewhat above or below half.
- Power tool: uniform $105 → net profit $10.5M. Two prices $120 (600,000 units) and $90 (600,000 more) → $24.0M, +129% net, +33.3% contribution. Fence failure: all buyers at $90 → 1.2M units × $30 = $36M contribution, $6M net, −43%. Three prices ($81.50 / $105 / $127.50) add only +12.5% on top of two.
- Coca-Cola can: highest point of sale is 5× the lowest (a 400% spread). Hot-weather cans at $3/$2/$1.40 for 1st/2nd/3rd unit: optimal uniform price is $2 (profit $3,000 vs $2,500 at $3 and $2,700 at $1.40), lower than the $2.50 cold-weather optimum.
- Lufthansa FRA–NYC: economy $734 vs first $8,950, a 1,218% gap; pre-1907 German rail four classes spanned ~1,000%. Promotions in FMCG/fashion reach 75% off. Sunshine Insurance refunds 25 EUR per rainy day, cap 200 EUR. Aerial tramway 20 EUR fair weather / 17 EUR bad.
- Beer (marginal utility $5/$4/$3/$2.50/$2, unit cost $0.50): nonlinear schedule yields $16.50 revenue, $14 contribution, average $3.30. Uniform $3.30 → 2 beers, $5.60 (−60%). Best uniform $2.50 → 4 beers, $8 (−43%). Uniform $3 or $2 → $7.50.
- Cinema: uniform 5.50 EUR, 9,000 visits, 49,500 EUR. Nonlinear 9.00 EUR (1st visit) down to 3.50 EUR (5th): 67,500 EUR (+37.7%), 11,000 visits, average 6.14 EUR. First-visit test: 9 EUR → 27,000; 10 EUR → 20,000; 12 EUR → 12,000.
- Wine & cheese (five buyers, zero cost): separate optima cheese $5 (profit $10) and wine $4 ($8) = $18. Pure bundle $5.50 → four buyers, $22 (+22.2%) despite a 39% bundle discount. Mixed bundling: bundle $5.50, wine $4, cheese $2.40 → $24.40 (+35.6%).
- Premium carmaker option packages (comfort/sports/safety): 21% bundle discount, +25% profit, plus lower logistics complexity.
- Couple travel: WTP $1,000 + $750; best uniform $750 → $1,500; couple price $1,750 → +16.7%.
- Volume discount (list $100, cost $60, 0/10/20/30% at 100/200/300 units, 300 sold): full-volume → $21,000 revenue, $3,000 profit; incremental → $24,000 (+14.3%), $6,000 (+100%).
- Orbitz: Mac users paid $20–30 more per night on a ~$100 average and booked 40% more 4–5-star rooms. Samoa Air: 92 cents per kg. UniCredit: 100 bp spread years 1–3, −10 bp per punctual year to a 70 bp floor (~$1,500/yr on a $500,000 mortgage).
- Distance test: $5 saving for a 20-minute drive — 68% go for a $15 item, 29% for a $125 item. Luxembourg gasoline ~20% below Germany draws drivers up to 50 miles. Kohlpharma parallel imports $760M (2012). ~25% of European auto profit comes from cross-country price differences.
- Parking garage: 2.50 EUR/h weekdays, 1 EUR Sundays, still empty. UK cinema chain: 25% weekday discounts moved nothing; one deep "cheap day" plus higher peak prices raised profit massively with a slight drop in visitors.
- Hilton Chicago (1,600 rooms): 13 empty at $100 vs a $110 rate with 50 empty — the second wins if ~1,550 pay; at 1,400 payers revenue drops to $154,000.
- Hi-Lo: promotions account for 70–80% of sales in beer, juice, household categories; lower-income buyers lean EDLP, higher-income Hi-Lo.
- Lexus LS400: launched 1989 at $35,000, 16,000 units year 1, 63,000 year 2; price +48% over six years. Failed in Germany where luxury price signals status.
- Penetration misfires: Audi Q7 55,000 EUR, 80,000 orders vs 70,000 capacity; Playmobil Ark 69.90 EUR resold at 84.09 EUR; Newnet 21.95 GBP raised 60% to 34.95 GBP after 600 users filled capacity; Asus eee 299 EUR met 10% of demand.
- iPhone 8 GB: $599 at launch (June 2007), cut to $399 within months; $100 gift cards to early buyers. FY2011/12: 125M units, $80.5B (~half of Apple revenue), average $640; build cost $118–245; after-tax return on sales 26.6% on $156.5B revenue.
- Nokia Lumia 900: $99 → $49.99 in three months; share price −64%. Sanofi Zaltrap: $11,063/month, then halved.
- Sunset of block booking: banned in the US in 1962. Leegin (2007) moved minimum retail pricing from per se illegal to rule of reason.

## Worked Example
Nonlinear cinema pricing. Three segments (A, B, C) each have a descending WTP for the 1st, 2nd, ... visit per month. Under one price the best is 5.50 EUR: A visits 2,000 times, B 3,000, C 4,000 — 9,000 visits, 49,500 EUR profit. Now price each visit separately. For the first visit, test candidate prices across segments: 9 EUR keeps all three segments and yields 27,000 EUR; 10 EUR loses A and drops to 20,000; 12 EUR keeps only C at 12,000 — so 9 EUR wins. Repeat for visits two through five; the schedule descends to 3.50 EUR for the fifth. Result: 67,500 EUR (+37.7%), 11,000 visits (+22%), and a higher average ticket (6.14 EUR) at the same time — impossible with one price on a downward-sloping curve. The fence is a named card stamped per visit, which is what the beer inn lacked.

## Key Takeaways
1. Before tuning a single price, estimate the triangle: the gain from structure (~2× with linear demand) dwarfs the gain from optimizing one number.
2. Never introduce a second price without a working fence; test the migration scenario (−43% in the power-tool case) before rollout.
3. Where the same buyer consumes multiple units, price the first unit highest and track usage per customer; nonlinear schedules can raise both volume and average price.
4. Bundle when item-level WTPs are heterogeneous; prefer mixed over pure bundling; re-optimize stand-alone prices (the cheese fell from $5 to $2.40) rather than leaving them at pre-bundle levels.
5. As seller, quote incremental volume discounts; the same rate table doubles profit versus full-volume.
6. Decide off-peak and dynamic prices from measured elasticity by period, not from occupancy; concentrate discounts into one deep, demand-moving slot rather than many shallow ones.
7. Stop adding price tiers when the incremental profit (33% → 12.5% → smaller) no longer covers the rising cost of information and access control.
