# Chapter 8: Innovations in Pricing

## Core Idea
Pricing innovation in the last three decades comes less from new price *levels* than from new price *structures*: change what you charge for (metric), when you charge (prepaid, pay per use), who sets the price (customer, auction), and how you split it (base + surcharge, free + premium). Each structure is a price-differentiation device in disguise, and each has a documented failure mode.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Pay Per Use / Pay-as-you-go**: charge for the performance the product delivers, not the product itself (GE/Rolls Royce sell thrust per hour; Michelin sells miles, not tires; Dürr+BASF charge per painted car; EnviroFalk per m³ treated; Orica per ton of blasted rock).
  - When to use: the product's value can be metered cheaply, the customer would rather have the outcome than the asset, and a step-change in product performance cannot be pushed through the old price anchor.
  - How: (1) identify the unit of customer benefit; (2) confirm a low-cost measurement/relay system exists; (3) install the asset at zero or low upfront cost; (4) price the metered unit so improved performance converts automatically into revenue; (5) bundle operation/maintenance into the per-unit price to move from product to service business.
- **New Price Metric**: keep the product, change the unit you invoice on (per ton vs per m³ vs per m² of finished wall; Hilti tool "fleet management" at a fixed monthly fee; SaaS per user per application).
  - When to use: your product wins on a dimension the current metric hides (lighter, faster to install, longer-lasting).
  - How: enumerate every feasible metric, compute your price gap versus competitors under each, pick the metric where your advantage shows; the more innovative the product and the stronger your position, the higher the odds customers accept the switch.
- **New Price Parameter (Sanifair)**: attach a price to something that was free, and soften it with a rebate that only buyers redeem.
  - When to use: a costly free service is consumed by non-buyers.
  - How: upgrade the service first, charge at the point of use, exempt protected groups (children, disabled), return part of the fee as a voucher usable only in your own stores. Result: free-riders pay full price, buyers pay a small net price.
- **Two-/Multidimensional Price Systems**: fixed component + variable usage component (industrial gas: daily bottle rent + price per kg; ARM: one-time license + per-chip royalty; Amazon Prime: annual fee unlocking service perks).
  - When to use: usage intensity varies widely across customers and you want one public price list that yields different effective prices.
  - How: set the fixed part so it is spread over volume; low-volume customers pay more per unit, high-volume less, with no visible discrimination.
- **Freemium**: free basic version + paid premium version; a specific form of penetration strategy suited to experience goods.
  - When to use: marginal cost near zero, value only becomes apparent through use.
  - How, three success factors: (1) an attractive basic offer that draws volume; (2) the right fencing (features, versions, or usage intensity) so first-timers convert; (3) a loyalty concept that turns first buyers into repeat buyers. Factors 1 and 2 trade off: too generous a free tier kills conversion, too stingy kills reach. Manage on two metrics: conversion rate and premium-customer lifetime value.
- **Flat Rate**: one fixed price per period, unlimited use. Simon's two theses (T-Mobile, 20 Nov 2000): light users subsidize heavy users; flat rates most likely lower revenue and profit.
  - When to use: consumption has a natural or artificial ceiling (a stomach at a buffet, a 1–3 hour window in a Tokyo bar).
  - How: before launching, obtain the light-vs-heavy user distribution and run rigorous simulations; if heavy users are numerous and usage is unbounded (data), do not offer it.
- **Prepaid**: customer pays before consuming (E-Plus prepaid cards, Starbucks stored-value cards, Zurich 30-day car insurance card in Mexico).
  - When to use: nonpayment risk matters, customers want budget control, or the market is less affluent.
  - How: accept a looser relationship than a contract; count breakage (unredeemed balances) as a profit line.
- **Customer-Driven Pricing / Name Your Own Price / Reverse Pricing**: customer bids, seller accepts if above a secret threshold (Priceline, IhrPreis.de, tallyman.de).
  - When to use: only to dispose of excess capacity without disturbing regular channels and list prices.
- **Pay What You Want**: buyer sets price, seller must accept (Radiohead In Rainbows; "suggested price" museums). Simon's verdict: avoid.
- **Profit-Oriented Incentive Systems**: tie sales commission to profit measures rather than revenue.
  - How: simplest form links commission to the discount granted (lower discount → higher commission); show the salesperson the commission move live on a PC or tablet during negotiation. Form and parameters matter less than the fact that profit, not revenue, drives variable pay.
- **Better Price Forecasts**: in commodity markets, forecast the price path and shift order timing.
  - How: model 30- and 90-day prices from supply/demand data plus salespeople's post-visit estimates; forecast up → "sell less now, push dates out"; forecast down → "sell more now, pull dates forward".
- **Intelligent Surcharges**, four categories: (1) unbundling a previously included item; (2) a new price component for something never priced; (3) passing on cost increases via an indexed surcharge; (4) price differentiation by time, place, or person.
  - When to use: base price is highly elastic and attention-grabbing, surcharges are low-attention and lower-elasticity; peak demand needs damping; extra value (space, speed, availability) can be metered.
- **À la Carte Pricing**: unbundle a pure bundle so customers buy components individually (iTunes single tracks; HBR articles at $6.95).
  - When to use: customers resent paying for the whole when they want two or three parts; introduce carefully because it forfeits the bundle's transfer of willingness to pay.
- **Auctions**: second-price (Vickrey) format induces bidders to reveal true maximum willingness to pay; "market design" tailors auction rules to goals beyond price extraction (viability, supply security).

## Key Concepts
- **Price transparency**: Internet and barcode-scanning apps collapse spatial fencing and raise cross-price elasticity for identical products.
- **Price anchor**: an accustomed price level customers resist deviating from; pay per use bypasses it by changing the unit.
- **Profit rectangle → profit triangle**: usage-based metrics let the seller capture more of the consumer surplus.
- **Fencing**: the feature, version, or usage-intensity gap that separates free from paid.
- **Penny gap**: the hardest step in freemium is moving a user from zero to any positive price.
- **Taxi-meter effect**: the disutility of watching charges accrue; flat rates remove it and act as insurance against out-of-pocket risk.
- **Flat rate trap**: unlimited plans cap revenue per customer while usage and network cost keep growing.
- **Breakage**: prepaid balances never redeemed, booked as profit.
- **Suggested price**: pay what you want with a hint.
- **Market design**: auction rules engineered for goals other than maximum price.

## Mental Models
- Think of every price structure as a price-differentiation mechanism: the same public offer, different effective prices by usage.
- Use a metric change when the product's improvement is real but a list-price increase of the same size would be refused; let the customer pay 25% more by using 25% more.
- Think of surcharges as low-attention prices: elasticity is lower there than on the headline price, but never on a fee that moves a customer from zero to non-zero for something felt as "already paid".
- Use flat rates only where physiology, time, or capacity caps consumption; otherwise treat them as a wager that heavy users are a small minority.

## Anti-patterns
- **Revenue-based sales commissions**: salespeople rationally chase revenue-max price, which sits far below profit-max price; discounts drift up.
- **Flat rates on unbounded consumption (data)**: telecoms locked their revenue ceiling while investing billions in capacity.
- **Pay what you want as a business model**: seller is at the buyer's mercy; worst with high variable costs (restaurants, hotels).
- **Name your own price for regular assortment**: attracts bargain hunters, bids come in unrealistically low; two of three pioneers vanished.
- **Zero-to-nonzero surcharges without research**: German public health insurers (8–10 €/month), Sixt (km over 200), Deutsche Bahn (2.50 € counter fee, withdrawn in two weeks), Bank of America ($5 debit fee, 20% more customer losses than the prior year). Members had no feel for the base price (deducted from payroll as a % of income) but felt the surcharge out of pocket.
- **Freemium free tier too rich or too poor**: one kills conversion, the other kills reach.

## Worked Example
Sales incentive redesign, power-tool business. Linear demand with maximum price $150 and variable unit cost $60. A salesperson paid on revenue targets the revenue-maximizing price of $75 (half the maximum price) and the company loses $7.5 M. A salesperson paid on profit targets $105 (midpoint between $150 and $60) and the company earns $10.5 M. Same product, same market, $18 M swing driven only by the compensation metric. Fix: keep the plan simple and confidential by paying commission on discount level rather than exposing margins; put the live commission figure on the rep's tablet during negotiation. Observed outcome across Simon-Kucher projects: discounts fall several points, volume unchanged, no customer defections.

## Key Takeaways
1. Before touching the price level, test whether a different metric (per outcome, per use, per period) captures your advantage better; compute the competitive gap under each metric.
2. Meter the outcome when a real performance gain (e.g., +25%) cannot survive as a +25% list price.
3. Freemium lives or dies on the free/paid usage gap and the penny gap; expect ~20% revenue upside from optimizing it, not from moving the price (elasticity ≈ 1).
4. Offer flat rates only with a consumption ceiling and after simulating the heavy-user tail; unbounded usage plus flat rate caps your revenue forever.
5. Move variable pay from revenue to profit (or discount) and show it live; discounts drop several points at zero volume cost.
6. Put price increases in low-attention surcharges tied to time, speed, space, or cost indices; never attach a fee to something customers feel is already paid for without prior market research.
7. Treat name-your-own-price as an inventory-clearing tool and pay-what-you-want as charity, not pricing.
