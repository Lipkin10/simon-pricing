# Chapter 5: Prices and Profits

## Core Idea
Profit is the only metric that sees both revenue and cost, so it is the only valid guide for pricing; and of the three profit drivers (price, volume, cost), price has the largest leverage and receives the least management attention.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three profit drivers**: Profit = (Price × Volume) − Cost. Every lever a company has maps to price, volume, or cost (split into variable and fixed).
  - When to use: before any "grow share" or "cut cost" initiative, to rank where a unit of effort pays most.
  - How: (1) take the P&L base case; (2) move each driver by the same % in isolation; (3) compare profit deltas; (4) put effort where the delta is biggest — in typical industrial cost structures that is price, then variable cost, then volume, then fixed cost.
- **Break-even analysis** (guard against "margin deception"): Break-even volume = Fixed costs ÷ (Price − Variable unit cost).
  - When to use: any proposed price cut, discount, promotion, free-shipping or tax-free scheme.
  - How: (1) compute contribution per unit at the new price; (2) compute the volume needed to hold profit constant; (3) ask honestly whether the market can supply that volume; (4) if the required increase is "gigantic", kill the move.
- **Source-of-demand test** (from the GM case): after any volume spike, ask where the buyers came from — new to category, taken from competitors, or pulled forward from your own future sales.
  - When to use: evaluating any promotion's apparent success.
  - How: track the months after the promotion; a mirror-image decline means you borrowed from the future.
- **Anti-discount incentive**: commission rises as the salesperson's granted discount falls.
  - When to use: to achieve a de facto price increase without a list-price change in competitive B2B markets.
  - How: rewire commission to reward realized price, not volume; measure average discount monthly.

## Key Concepts
- **Return on sales / net margin**: profit per $100 of revenue; single digits are the norm, not the 33–46% consumers imagine.
- **Contribution margin**: price minus variable unit cost (in retail: retail price minus wholesale price); profit appears only when total contribution exceeds fixed costs.
- **Cost-plus pricing**: marking up cost by a fixed %; ignores customer value and volume response, so it can price you out of the market or leave money on the table.
- **Price elasticity**: % change in volume ÷ % change in price, sign dropped; elasticity 2 means a 1% cut adds 2% volume.
- **Advertising / sales-force elasticity**: same ratio applied to ad spend or sales investment; an order of magnitude smaller than price elasticity.
- **Pantry effect**: customers stock up during a deal and stop buying afterwards; the discount trains them to wait for the next deal.
- **Borrowing from the future**: a promotion that pulls forward purchases that would have happened anyway at full price.
- **"Price before Volume"**: Lanxess's 2005 motto; the operational form of profit-first pricing.
- **Margin deception**: a high per-unit margin that hides a collapsing or unattainable volume requirement.

## Mental Models
- Think of every discount as the gardener does: at a 6% margin, a 3% discount means doing twice the work for the same money. Translate any concession into "how much more must I sell".
- Use the break-even volume as the first filter on a price cut; the spontaneous answer ("cut 20%, sell 20% more") is almost always wrong by a factor of several.
- Think of price as the only marketing instrument with zero upfront investment and near-instant effect — and therefore also the one competitors neutralize fastest.
- When a metric other than profit (share, traffic, revenue, capacity utilization) becomes the real goal, expect pricing to drift toward zero; the cheapest way to win share is to give the product away.

## Anti-patterns
- **"We'll make it up on volume"**: with a 40% contribution margin, a 20% price cut needs +100% volume; consumer goods rarely have that elasticity.
- **Market share as the operative goal**: TV makers lost $13 billion collectively in 2012; the 2013 German pharma-wholesale price war (Phoenix vs Noweda, Celesio) left shares unchanged and destroyed margins.
- **Accepting concessions bigger than the margin**: an engineering firm gave an extra 17% discount on a $10 million order priced at a 14% margin; the same habit put it in bankruptcy five years later.
- **Celebrating traffic and revenue while profit falls**: pet-supply e-tailer with sales +30% and +34% in consecutive quarters posted a loss; a ceramics company grew revenue 4% and lost 28% of profit (2013).
- **Cost-plus as the pricing method**: guarantees nothing about profit because it never asks what the customer values or how volume reacts.
- **Extending a promotion that is already cannibalizing**: GM kept its employee-price program running through September 2005 while sales were plunging in August.
- **Spending 70% of management time on cost, 20% on volume, 10% on price**: the exact inverse of each driver's profit leverage.

### Profit-driver simulation (power tools, Simon-Kucher case, rounded)
Base: price $100, variable cost $60, fixed cost $30M, volume 1M units → revenue $100M, cost $90M, profit $10M (10% margin).

| Driver improved by 5% in isolation | New figure | Profit | Δ profit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price +5% | $105 | $15.0M | **+50%** |
| Variable cost −5% | $57 | $13.0M | **+30%** |
| Volume +5% | 1.05M units | $12.0M | **+20%** |
| Fixed cost −5% | $28.5M | $11.5M | **+15%** |

### Price cut arithmetic (same case)
| Scenario | Contribution/unit | Volume | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base | $40 | 1.0M | $10M |
| Price −20% ($80), volume unchanged | $20 | 1.0M | −$10M |
| Price −20%, volume +20% (managers' guess) | $20 | 1.2M | −$6M (loss) |
| Price −20%, profit held at $10M | $20 | **2.0M (+100%)** | $10M |

Derived rule: required volume increase = cut% ÷ (contribution margin% − cut%). For a 40% margin and a 20% cut → 20 ÷ 20 = 100%.

### Break-even volume
| Price | Contribution | Break-even units |
|---|---|---|
| $80 | $20 | 1,500,000 |
| $100 | $40 | 750,000 |
| $120 | $60 | 500,000 |

### 2% price increase, volume constant (Fortune Global 500, FY2012 data)
| Company | Profit change |
|---|---|
| Sony | ×2.36 (more than doubles) |
| Walmart | +41.4% |
| General Motors | +36.8% |
| P&G, Samsung Electronics, Nestlé | >+10% |
| IBM, Apple | modest but significant |

Derived rule: Δprofit% ≈ price change% ÷ net margin% (volume constant). The lower the margin, the more explosive the leverage — and the more dangerous a cut.

Real-world 2% increase: $14B industrial company added an anti-discount commission; average discount fell from 16% to 14% in three months, no volume loss, no defections.

### Elasticities
| Instrument | Elasticity |
|---|---|
| Price (tens of thousands of products) | 1.3–3, median ≈2 |
| Advertising | 0.05–0.1 |
| Sales force | 0.20–0.35 |
| Price vs advertising | 10–20× |
| Price vs sales force | 7–8× |
| Special offers (e.g. GM program), extreme | up to 10 |

Equivalence: a 1% price change ≈ a 10–20% change in ad budget ≈ a 7–8% change in sales-force investment.

- Lanxess "Price before Volume" since 2005: EBITDA $600M → $1.5B, CAGR >14% from 2004.
- Amazon share price +50% in 2015 despite thin profit.

## Worked Example
GM, spring 2005. Sales are down 7.4% (April) and 4.7% (May). Marketing sells every vehicle at the employee price — defined as what a dealer pays — from 1 June for four months. June jumps 41.4%, July 19.8%; GM fears running out of cars. Ford and Chrysler copy in July. Apply the source-of-demand test: a car is not a pantry item, so the extra buyers were not new demand — they were people who would have bought later at full price. August sales fall, September −23.9%, October −22.7%; monthly volume halves from ~600k to <300k. GM nonetheless extends the program to end-September. Cost: $3,623 average discount per vehicle, a $10.5B loss for the year, market cap down from $20.9B to $12.5B in four months, residual values damaged. Lutz's later verdict: four million cars at a profit beat five million at zero. Lesson: a volume spike with no new demand source is a loan against the future, repaid with interest.

## Key Takeaways
1. Rank initiatives by profit leverage: in a typical industrial structure, +5% price gives +50% profit; the same effort on volume gives +20%.
2. Before any price cut, compute break-even volume; require the answer to be plausible against known elasticities (median ≈2), not hopeful.
3. Translate every concession into "how much more must I sell": at a 6% margin, 3% off means double the work.
4. Prefer disguised price increases (discount discipline, anti-discount commissions) over list-price moves in competitive markets: 16% → 14% average discount is a 2% price rise with zero volume loss.
5. Treat traffic, revenue, share and utilization as secondary; if profit is not the governing metric, pricing decays toward giving the product away.
6. After any promotion, audit the following months for the mirror-image dip; pulled-forward demand is not growth.
7. Reject cost-plus as a method: it ignores customer value and volume response, the two things pricing must be built on.
